PC Build Lab

Build a desktop PC on a virtual bench: ground yourself, drag every part into the right slot, connect the right cables, then press the power button and see whether it passes POST. Then choose compatible parts for a client's budget.

What each component does

Motherboard — the main circuit board. Everything plugs into it; it carries power and data between them. The chipset adds extra USB, SATA and PCIe lanes.

CPU — the processor that runs instructions (fetch–decode–execute). Specs: cores/threads, clock speed (GHz), socket, TDP (heat in watts), integrated graphics or not.

CPU cooler + thermal paste — paste fills the tiny gaps; heat pipes carry heat to fins; a fan blows it away.

RAM — fast, volatile working memory for the programs that are open right now. Lost when the power goes off.

Storage — non-volatile: keeps the OS, programs and files when the power is off (HDD, SATA SSD, NVMe SSD).

Graphics card (GPU) — renders the picture and does heavy parallel maths. Optional if the CPU has integrated graphics.

Power supply (PSU) — converts 230 V AC mains into 12 V, 5 V and 3.3 V DC.

Case + fans — hold and protect everything and move air through it: in at the front, out at the rear/top.

SSDs have no moving parts, so they are silent, shock-resistant and much faster at random reads. HDDs are still cheapest per terabyte.

RAM channels

Each channel is a separate 64-bit path to the CPU. Two sticks on different channels (A + B) can be read at the same time — dual channel doubles the bandwidth. Two sticks on the same channel (A1 + A2) run in single channel.

With two sticks the manual says A2 and B2: the slots at the end of each channel's wiring give the cleanest signal.

PSU wattage & 80 PLUS

Size it: add up the CPU TDP + GPU power + about 50 W for the board, RAM and fans, then add 30% headroom for power spikes and to keep the PSU near its efficient middle range.

e.g. 65 W CPU + 115 W GPU + 50 W = 230 W → 230 × 1.3 = 299 W minimum
80 PLUS ratingEfficiency at 50% load*
White (standard)80%
Bronze85%

Gold90%
Platinum92%
Titanium94%

*Figures for 115 V; on 230 V UK mains they are slightly higher. The wasted power becomes heat — a 90% PSU delivering 450 W draws 500 W from the wall.

ESD & safety

  • Electrostatic discharge (ESD) — you can carry thousands of volts without feeling it; under ~3 000 V you won't feel a spark, but chips can be damaged by far less.
  • Wear an anti-static wrist strap clipped to bare, unpainted metal on the case, and work on an anti-static mat.
  • Handle cards and sticks by the edges; never touch the gold contacts or the CPU pins. Keep parts in their anti-static bags until needed.
  • Unplug mains / switch the PSU to O before working inside. Never open a PSU — its capacitors hold a charge.
  • The strap has a 1 MΩ resistor so a mains fault can't send a large current through you — but still take it off before connecting mains.

POST & debug LEDs

POST (power-on self-test) is the firmware checking the hardware before booting. Many boards have four debug LEDs; the one that stays lit shows where it stopped.

LED stays onUsual causes
CPUNo CPU power (8-pin EPS), CPU not seated, bent pins, no cooler/overheat
DRAMNo RAM, RAM not clicked in, wrong type
VGANo graphics: "F" CPU with no GPU, GPU missing its PCIe power, card not seated
BOOTNo bootable drive found / drive not connected

Build order

  1. Wrist strap on, PSU switched off.
  2. I/O shield, then standoffs, then motherboard.
  3. Open lever → CPU (match triangles) → close lever.
  4. Thermal paste → cooler (fan to CPU_FAN).
  5. RAM (A2 + B2), M.2 SSD.
  6. GPU in the top x16 slot.
  7. PSU, drives, fans.
  8. Cables: 24-pin, 8-pin CPU, PCIe, SATA power + data, front panel.
  9. Power on → POST → UEFI → boot from USB → install the OS.
Many technicians fit the CPU, cooler and RAM to the board outside the case first — it's easier to reach. This lab does it in the case so you can see each step.

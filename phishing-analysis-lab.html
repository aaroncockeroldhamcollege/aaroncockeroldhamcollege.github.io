Phishing Analysis Lab

Work an inbox like a security analyst. Hover links to reveal where they really go, check attachments safely, open the raw message headers and read SPF, DKIM and DMARC, then decide: phishing or legitimate? Report the phish, mark the genuine mail as safe, and write it up. Every organisation, person and domain here is fictional.

The red-flag checklist

One flag doesn't prove phishing; three or four together nearly always do. Slow down when you see them.

🎭

The name doesn't match the address. The display name is just text. "Helen Marsh (CEO)" at freemail.example is not your CEO.

🔎

Look-alike or cousin domains. oakfie1d.test (digit 1), 0akfield.test (zero), brightwatter.test (extra t), oakfield-hr.example (extra word).

🔗

The link text lies. Hover (don't click) and read the real destination in the status bar. The text can say anything, even a genuine-looking URL.

🔑

Asks for a password, code or "re-validation". IT never needs your password by email. Go to known sites by typing the address yourself.

⏰

Urgency or threats. "Within 24 hours", "account suspended", "legal action", "clamped". Panic stops you thinking.

💷

Money: new bank details, gift cards, urgent transfers. Always verify by phone using a number you already have, never the one in the email.

🤫

Secrecy or skipping the process. "Keep this between us", "don't call, I'm in meetings", "I'll authorise it later".

📎

Risky attachments. Double extensions (invoice.pdf.exe), macro documents (.docm, .xlsm), archives (.zip) or scripts (.js, .htm).

📱

QR codes. They hide the link and move you to your phone, where you can't hover and work protections don't apply.

🎁

Unexpected or too good to be true. A prize you didn't enter, an invoice from a company you don't use, a file you weren't expecting.

🎯

Personal details used to build trust. Spear-phishers read your social media: your race time, your team, your charity. Personal doesn't mean genuine.

👤

Generic greeting. "Dear user", "Dear customer" from someone who should know your name.

SPF, DKIM and DMARC in one picture

ResultWhat it tells you
spf=passThe sending IP is allowed to send for the envelope (Return-Path) domain.
spf=softfail / failThe IP is not on the list: a strong sign of a forged sender.
dkim=pass d=…Signed by that domain and not changed in transit. Check that d= matches From.
dmarc=failNothing lined up with the From domain. With p=none the mail is still delivered.
Passing isn't the same as safe. A look-alike domain (oakfie1d.test) or a webmail account passes all three, because the attacker owns it. The checks prove which domain sent it, not whether that domain is honest.

Read a link from the right

The registered domain is the part just before the ending. Everything to its left is a sub-domain, which the owner can set to anything.

https://password.oakfield.test.account-verify.example/reset?id=j.reid

Owned by account-verify.example. "password.oakfield.test" is just a sub-domain they created to fool you.

https://learning.oakfield.test/course/sec-2026

Owned by oakfield.test, the genuine domain.

Attachments that bite

FileWhy it's risky
invoice.pdf.exeWindows hides the real .exe; it's a program.
.docm / .xlsmMacro documents: "Enable Content" runs code.
.zip / .isoHide the real file from email filters.
.js / .htm / .htmlScripts or fake sign-in pages that open in a browser.

How to report

  1. Don't click, reply, forward to colleagues or open attachments.
  2. Press Report phishing on the ribbon. It sends the full message, with headers, to the security team and removes it from your inbox.
  3. No button? Forward it as an attachment to the security mailbox so the headers survive.
  4. For a text message, send a screenshot to IT. Many countries also have a short code for reporting spam texts.
  5. If it's genuine but you were unsure, Mark as safe or ask the sender on a different channel, such as a phone number you already have.
Analysts look at the headers to find the sending infrastructure, block the domain and IPs, and search every mailbox for the same message.

What to do if you clicked

  1. Tell IT straight away. Minutes matter. You won't be in trouble for reporting; you might be for hiding it.
  2. Entered a password? Change it now from a device you trust, and anywhere else you use it. Expect MFA prompts you didn't start, and deny them.
  3. Opened an attachment or enabled macros? Disconnect from the network (Wi-Fi off or cable out) but leave the PC on for IT.
  4. Paid or changed bank details? Ring your bank and the finance team immediately; fast action can recall a payment.
  5. IT will check for mailbox forwarding rules, unusual sign-ins and other people who received it.
